This work presentsSHDFL, a Secure Hierarchical Decentralized Federated
Learning stack that combines the Bonawitz et al. [1] secure aggregation protocol

pull-based dissemination, letting nodes continue local training while periodi-

served even when rounds abort.

2 Related Work
The integration of blockchain with decentralized federated learning (DFL) has
gained significant attention as a mechanism to enhance trust, auditability, and
coordination in distributed learning systems. Formery et al. [2] provide a com-
prehensive architectural analysis of Blockchain-Based Decentralized Federated
Learning (BDFL), decomposing such systems into layered components including
blockchain infrastructure, client interconnection, consensus management, and
data transmission. Their work highlights decentralization, immutability, and
transparency as key properties that enable trustworthy coordination in open and
partially trusted environments. By anchoring model commitments and learning
events on-chain, BDFL architectures improve traceability and tamper resistance.
However, these frameworks primarily focus on architectural characterization
rather than optimization-aware learning design. The learning layer is typically
treated independently from the blockchain layer, with limited consideration of
communication topology, data heterogeneity, or aggregation structure. More-
over, most BDFL designs assume a flat peer-to-peer network without structured
hierarchy. Our work extends this coordination paradigm by tightly integrat-
ing blockchain-based auditability with topology-aware decentralized optimiza-
tion and multi-level aggregation.

A fundamental challenge in decentralized stochastic gradient descent (D-
SGD) is convergence under heterogeneous (non-IID) data distributions. The
D-Cliques approach [3] addresses this issue by organizing nodes into densely
connected cliques whose aggregated label distributions approximate the global
distribution, while maintaining sparse inter-clique connectivity to reduce com-
munication cost. This topology mitigates optimization bias induced by hetero-
geneous neighborhoods and improves convergence stability.
Le Bars et al. [4] further refine the theoretical understanding of D-SGD by in-
troducing the notion ofneighborhood heterogeneity. Their analysis demonstrates
that convergence depends not only on global heterogeneity but also on the mis-
match between a node’s data distribution and that of its neighbors. By explicitly
coupling topology and heterogeneity, they show that appropriate topology learn-
ingcansignificantlyreduceconvergencedegradation.Theseworksprovidestrong
theoretical and practical motivation for topology-aware decentralized training.
Nevertheless, both D-Cliques and refined topology learning concentrate on
improving convergence within a single-level network and do not address system-
level concerns such as verifiable coordination, privacy-preserving aggregation
across administrative domains, or hierarchical scalability. In contrast, our frame-
work extends topology-aware principles into a structured hierarchy composed of
clusters, states, and a nation-level aggregation layer. At the lowest level, nodes
are grouped into statistically balanced clusters inspired by D-Cliques. Multiple
clusters form a state, and multiple states contribute to nation-level aggregation.
This hierarchy enables scalable coordination across organizational boundaries
while preserving topology-aware convergence benefits.
Our topology also allowed us to address privacy, which remains critical in
decentralized learning. Bonawitz et al. [1] propose a practical secure aggregation
protocol that allows an aggregator to compute only the sum of client updates us-
ing pairwise masking and secret sharing, ensuring robustness to dropouts while
preventing exposure of individual updates. Although highly effective, this pro-
tocol is typically applied in centralized federated learning with a trusted server.
Our framework adapts secure aggregation to the cluster level of a decentral-
ized hierarchy. Within each cluster, a designated cluster head coordinates secure
aggregation without accessing individual model updates. At higher levels, aggre-
gation operates on already aggregated cluster models, reducing privacy exposure
whileenablingscalablelearning.Thislayeredaggregationstrategypreservescon-
fidentiality while supporting large-scale deployment.
Scalability challenges in blockchain-based federated learning have also moti-
vatedoff-chainstoragesolutions.Zhaoetal.[5]proposeleveragingtheInterPlan-
etary File System (IPFS) to store model parameters off-chain, while anchoring
contentidentifiers(CIDs)andhashesontheblockchain.Thisseparationbetween

However, existing blockchain-FL systems often adopt flat orchestration mod-
els and lack structured multi-level aggregation. Our framework incorporates
the blockchain/IPFS separation paradigm within a three-level hierarchy: cluster

Prior work typically advances one dimension at a time—blockchain coordi-

storage [5]—and leaves the others untouched. Our contribution is to unify these
components in a cross-silo setting with stable identities and administrative au-

auditability, and scalability simultaneously by coupling topology-aware decen-
tralized learning with secure aggregation and blockchain-anchored coordination
across all layers, moving blockchain-based decentralized FL closer to practical
large-scale deployment.

Label-Balanced cluster Topology and Membership
The TTP plans topology before training begins. After partitioning the dataset
with a Dirichlet sampler, it computes per-node label histograms and feeds them
to a topology builder. The builder repeatedly swaps members across candidate
clusters to minimize label skew, producing roughly balanced clusters that rep-
resent the global distribution. Inter-cluster edges are then laid out using a con-
figurable mode (ring, ring with extra edges, small-world, or similar), yielding
bridge nodes that later disseminate ECMs. Thresholds for each cluster are set

Secure Aggregation Procedure in Cluster Rounds
Each cluster-level training round executes the Secure Aggregation Procedure
(SAP) so that the elected aggregator learns only the average of the members’
model deltas while every contributor’s raw tensor remains unknown. Without
SAP, trainers would have to upload their plaintext updates to the aggregator,
enabling a curious or compromised aggregator to infer label distributions or
memorized samples from individual gradients. Embedding SAP directly into the
cluster orchestration preserves the privacy boundary proved in the Bonawitz et
al. [1] protocol while keeping the rest of the federated pipeline unchanged.
SAP requires a quorum at every round boundary because the server can only
reconstruct the pairwise masks and self-masks after it gathers at leasttdistinct
shares for each secret. In practice, the aggregator waits for messages from the
surviving clients in each of the four rounds and aborts if the threshold is not
met, mirroring the“abort if|u i|< t”guard described in the paper. The quorum
therefore guarantees that (i) no coalition smaller thantcan strip another client’s
mask and (ii) the unmasking phase has sufficient information to produce a valid
mean; without it, the round is deliberately canceled to avoid revealing partial
information or emitting an incorrect aggregate.
When a cluster round aborts due to insufficient survivors, all nodes reset that
cluster round pipeline: they resume local SGD from the checkpoint produced in
the failed attempt, re-run message advertisement, and execute SAP again. This
restart loop can repeat multiple times, which effectively increases the amount of
local computation associated with a single logical cluster round (Round Ampli-
fication). The system tolerates this amplification because it trades extra local
epochsforthesafetyguaranteethatonlyquorum-protectedaggregateseverleave
the clique.

Hierarchy Structure
Above the cluster layer, the system deploys a configurable hierarchy whose levels
(cluster, state, nation, etc.) are declared through structured configuration. Each
level has a scope name, identifier, timer interval, wait budget, merge policy,
and fan-out quota. Nodes also ingest a hierarchy map that lists the roster and
children of every scope, enabling them to derive their memberships at runtime.
During startup, every node instantiates a per-scope runtime that tracks round
queues, round-robin pools for aggregator candidates, and the fan-out members
appointed to relay models upward.
High-level aggregation is fully time-driven: each scope owns a wall-clock in-
terval, and when its timer fires the member nodes enqueue a round while briefly
pausinglocaloptimizationfortheconfiguredwaitwindow.Ifanodeistheelected
aggregator, it receives the latest model artifacts from its child scopes via bridge
fan-out, de-duplicates them per child, fetches the referenced tensors from stor-
age, and aggregate the collected model artifacts. Only one candidate commits
the result per round: the merged tensor is published to IPFS, anchored on the
ledger under the scope identifier, and the commit event is logged for observ-
ability. Peers simply watch the ledger to detect completion and mark the round
satisfied without running their own merge.
Dissemination back to the lower layers follows an asynchronous pull model.
Once the wait window elapses—or when a fresh anchor arrives early—nodes
querytheledger,fetchthereferencedtensorfromIPFS,verifyitshash,andapply
the scope’s policy (replace, interpolate with a configuredα, etc.) before resum-
ing local updates. Each scope records the last applied CID so redundant anchors
are ignored, allowing nodes to adopt the most recent confirmed checkpoint even
while the next one is being constructed. Because pauses are brief and higher-

cation combine to deliver consistent multi-level coordination without exposing
individual model updates.

To protect the confidentiality of local model updates, SHDFL integrates the
Practical Secure Aggregation Protocol proposed by Bonawitz et al. [1] within
each cluster aggregation step. In this design, trainers never transmit their raw
model updates directly to the cluster aggregator. Instead, each update is crypto-
graphically masked before transmission, ensuring that individual contributions
remain hidden during aggregation.

To maintain correctness in the presence of node dropouts, SHDFL adopts the
secret-sharing mechanism from the original protocol. Mask seeds are distributed
using a threshold secret sharing scheme, allowing the remaining participants to
reconstruct the necessary information to remove residual masks if some nodes
fail during the protocol execution. This prevents incomplete masking from cor-
rupting the aggregation result.
Within SHDFL, secure aggregation is executed locally inside each cluster of
the decentralized topology. As a result, the cluster aggregator learns only the
aggregated update and cannot infer any individual gradient. Combined with de-
centralized communication and cluster-based training, this design ensures that
sensitive local training information remains confidential while still enabling col-
laborative model optimization.
5 Summary
Two directions warrant further investigation. First, SAP imposes a sizable band-
width premium because masked payloads expand well beyond plain-text tensors;
we plan to prototype packet-level compression tailored to masked gradients so
cluster rounds keep their privacy guarantees while shrinking the transmissions.
Second, the system currently assumes static cluster assignments, so permanent
dropouts can strand a cluster at SAP. We therefore intend to design mid-run re-
balancing that detects underpopulated cliques and redistributes surviving nodes
withoutbreakingthedeterministicidentifiers,allowingclusterstoregainquorum
without operator intervention.
